When AI Learns
to Make Music —
Who Pays?

AI companies trained their music generators on millions of songs. The musicians who made those songs didn't consent, weren't paid, and are now competing with the result. Here's how it works — and why it matters.

Step 1 — The Library

Before an AI can make music, it needs to hear an enormous amount of it. Companies collect millions of recordings — from streaming platforms, YouTube, music licensing sites, and other online sources. We're talking about more music than any person could listen to in several lifetimes.

Here's the problem: most of those songs were taken without asking the artists. The companies argue this is no different from a musician listening to records for inspiration. Artists disagree — and that disagreement is at the heart of every lawsuit in this space.

Plain English

Imagine a cooking school that secretly recorded every great chef's recipes without permission, and then used those recipes to train a robot chef that could now compete with the original chefs for work.

Step 2 — Turning Sound into Numbers

Computers can't process audio the way our ears do. So every song gets converted into a kind of visual map — showing which frequencies are playing at which moments. Think of it like a very detailed sheet music, but instead of notes on a staff, it's a grid of numbers.

This is how the AI "reads" music. A three-minute song becomes millions of tiny data points. The computer doesn't hear a melody — it sees a pattern of numbers, and it gets extremely good at recognizing which number tends to follow which.

Plain English

It's like converting a painting into a massive spreadsheet of color values. You lose the feeling, but you can analyze the patterns mathematically.

Step 3 — Learning the Patterns

Here's where the actual "learning" happens. The AI is shown billions of these number sequences and asked to guess: "given everything before this moment, what comes next?" Every time it's wrong, it adjusts. Every time it's right, it reinforces that approach.

After enough repetitions — across millions of songs — the AI develops an incredibly detailed internal model of how music works. It learns that a certain chord tends to resolve a certain way, that a hip-hop beat has a different rhythmic feel than jazz, that some vocal timbre usually appears with certain production aesthetics.

Plain English

It's like learning a language by reading billions of sentences. You didn't memorize them — but you absorbed the grammar, the vocabulary, the rhythm of how words flow together. Now you can write new sentences in any style.

Step 4 — Taking Orders

A raw AI model that just predicts "what comes next" isn't very useful for a consumer product. So companies add a second layer of training: teaching the model to follow text descriptions. You type "lo-fi hip-hop, rainy afternoon" and it should generate exactly that.

This is done by pairing thousands of songs with written descriptions, and training the model to connect the language to the sound. Human reviewers also rate the outputs and the model learns to produce results that sound better to human ears.

Plain English

Think of it like training a skilled musician who already knows a hundred genres, to take requests from anyone — even people who can't describe music very precisely.

Step 5 — The Output

When you type a prompt into Suno, Udio, or a similar tool, you get back a brand-new audio file. The AI isn't playing back any specific song from its training data — it's generating something new, built from the patterns it absorbed across millions of recordings.

This is the key technical claim companies make in their legal defense: the model doesn't store or reproduce songs; it learned from them. Critics counter: the capability was built on those songs without permission, and the output competes directly with the people who made them.

The core tension

The output is genuinely new. But is "new" the same as "fair"? That's the question courts are now deciding.

They used our work — and didn't pay
The economic argument
▾

Songs are commercial products. Musicians, songwriters, and labels invest real money and time making them. When an AI company vacuums up millions of those songs to train a product it then sells to consumers, it's extracting value without paying for it.

Compare it to any other business: if a company needed steel to build a factory, it couldn't just take steel from other businesses without paying. AI companies, in effect, took the raw material of music — recordings — and used them to build a competing product.

Think of it this way A food delivery app secretly collected every restaurant's recipes, trained its own AI chef on them, and now sells that chef's cooking at half the price. The original restaurants never agreed to this — and now they have a new competitor built entirely on their work.
🎭
Anyone can now copy my sound
The identity argument
▾

Copyright law protects specific recordings — but not a musician's style. That means anyone can type "make music in the style of [artist name]" and get something that sounds remarkably like them. The artist has no legal say in this, and earns nothing from it.

For many artists, their sound is the product of decades of development. It's their most valuable creative asset. Having it turned into a free menu option — available to anyone — feels like a fundamental violation, even if technically legal.

Think of it this way Imagine if someone could create an AI that writes in your voice, uses your mannerisms, and sells essays that sound exactly like you — without your permission and with no credit or payment to you.
📉
It's taking away real jobs
The labor argument
▾

There are whole categories of music work that are now under direct competitive pressure from AI: background music for videos, podcasts, apps, and retail spaces; theme music for small productions; custom jingles; stock music libraries. These were steady income sources for working musicians.

These aren't the famous artists — these are the session players, the composers, the freelancers who make a living from the music industry's less glamorous work. They're the ones being displaced first and most severely.

Think of it this way It's not that AI is replacing Taylor Swift. It's replacing the person who composed the hold music for your dentist's office, the background track for a YouTube cooking video, or the score for a local theater production.
🙈
We weren't asked. We didn't consent.
The fairness argument
▾

Most musicians have no idea whether their music was used in AI training data. There was no announcement, no opt-in, no consent form. The companies just took what they needed, built their products, and are now worth billions of dollars.

Some AI companies now offer "opt-out" lists — artists can apply to have their music excluded. But critics say this gets the ethical burden exactly backward. You shouldn't have to opt out of something you never agreed to join in the first place.

Think of it this way Imagine someone used your photo in their advertisement without asking, then, when called out, offered you the option to "opt out" of future ads. The original wrong — using it without permission — remains unaddressed.

And even when labels do reach settlements with AI companies, independent artists are often left out. Irving Azoff, the legendary manager who founded the Music Artists Coalition, put it plainly after the first wave of label deals: "We've seen this before — everyone talks about 'partnership,' but artists end up on the sidelines with scraps." Artists, he argued, must have creative control, fair compensation, and clarity about deals being done based on their catalogs.

🌊
It could make all music sound the same
The cultural argument
▾

AI models are trained on what already exists. By definition, they can only recombine patterns from the past — they can't have genuine new experiences, can't be heartbroken for the first time, can't grow up in a specific neighborhood with a specific cultural identity. The fear is that flooding the market with AI music will push human musicians to compete on price rather than originality — and the music that benefits culturally from risk-taking will become harder to fund.

There's also a specific concern about cultural appropriation at scale. Genres like blues, cumbia, Afrobeat, and flamenco were developed by specific communities over generations. AI can extract those patterns and offer them as free menu options — without any of the licensing or royalty flows that have historically (imperfectly) returned some value to those communities.

Part Three
Who's Right,
Legally?

The honest answer: nobody knows yet. Courts are only beginning to rule on this. Here are the two main arguments being made.

The Tech Companies Say:

Learning isn't copying. The AI didn't make copies of songs to sell — it studied them, the way a music student studies recordings. No individual song is stored inside the model.
The output is genuinely new. Every song the AI generates is an original creation — it isn't playing back any training track.
This is how all creative learning works. Human musicians also learn by listening to lots of music. Copyright law has never required musicians to get permission before being influenced.
"Fair use" covers transformative work. American copyright law allows some uses of protected work when the result is transformative — courts have accepted this argument for search engines and other technologies.
vs

The Artists & Labels Say:

Training is a commercial act, not study. A student isn't building a product they'll sell for billions of dollars. The scale and commercial purpose changes everything.
"New" doesn't mean "fair." The AI's entire capability was built on copyrighted work. The creators of that work see no benefit while a competitor profits directly from it.
AI can reproduce songs too closely. Tests have shown AI models can generate audio that sounds nearly identical to specific training tracks — suggesting the material is closer to stored than simply "learned from."
The legal pressure is already working. Suno agreed to phase out its current AI models entirely and replace them with fully licensed ones as part of its Warner settlement — a meaningful concession that wouldn't have happened without the threat of massive damages.
What's Happened So Far
The Fight in Court
Some labels are settling — but not all, and independent artists have now filed their own suits. The legal picture is shifting fast, with no court ruling yet on the core question.
2023
The record labels start sounding the alarm
Sony, Universal, and Warner — the three biggest labels in the world — begin lobbying Congress and preparing legal strategy, arguing that AI music companies are effectively stealing their catalogs.
June 2024
The major labels file lawsuits
All three majors sue both Suno and Udio in federal court, alleging copyright infringement at a scale of potentially hundreds of thousands of recordings. The potential damages — up to $150,000 per violation — are enormous.
October – November 2025
Udio settles with Universal and Warner
Universal Music Group settles with Udio first, announcing a plan for a new jointly built "licensed and protected" service to launch in 2026. Warner follows with its own Udio deal shortly after. Both settlements mean Udio users can no longer download their creations — a change that sparks user backlash.
November 19, 2025
Suno raises $250M — valued at $2.45 billion
Despite the active lawsuits, investors led by Menlo Ventures and Nvidia's venture arm pour $250 million into Suno at a $2.45 billion valuation. Suno simultaneously announces $200 million in annual revenue. The message from the investment community is clear: they're betting the legal fights will resolve through licensing deals, not shut the company down.
November 25, 2025
Warner settles with Suno — and becomes a partner
Warner Music Group drops its lawsuit against Suno and announces a full partnership to build "next-generation licensed AI music." As part of the deal, Suno commits to phasing out its current AI models in 2026 and replacing them with new, fully licensed ones. Suno also acquires Songkick — the concert discovery platform — from WMG. Sony and Universal are still in litigation with Suno.
2025 — ongoing
Independent artists fight back on their own
The major label deals don't speak for everyone. Country artist Anthony Justice files suit against both Suno and Udio on behalf of a group of independent musicians, alleging their recordings were used without permission. A second class action, led by musician David Woulard, follows in October. These cases — focused on independent artists rather than major label catalogs — are in early stages as of early 2026.
2026 and beyond
Congress considers new rules — no law yet
Lawmakers are weighing legislation that would protect artists from AI-generated voice and likeness replication. No comprehensive AI music law has passed. Courts haven't ruled on the core "fair use" question yet. The legal architecture of AI-generated music remains genuinely unsettled.
This explainer covers events through March 2026. Legal cases and valuations described are active and subject to change.
